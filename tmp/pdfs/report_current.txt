Abstract
Forecasting economic development indicators remains an important problem in data
science, economics, and public policy. Among macroeconomic indicators, GDP per capita is
especially important because it reflects average economic output relative to population size
and therefore provides a more interpretable proxy for living standards than total GDP alone.
However, GDP per capita is shaped by multiple interacting demographic, social, and
macroeconomic factors, including population dynamics, life expectancy, inflation,
unemployment, digital access, and exposure to major global shocks. This project
investigates whether these indicators can be integrated into an interpretable forecasting
framework for modelling future GDP per capita at the country level.
The study uses open country-level datasets derived primarily from World Bank sources,
including GDP per capita, population, life expectancy, inflation, unemployment, internet
usage, and country metadata. These datasets were cleaned, standardised, and merged into
a country-year panel. The final extended panel contains 11,373 observations, 25 variables,
214 countries, 7 World Bank regions, and the period from 1960 to 2023. In addition to the
merged panel, separate univariate time-series models were developed for GDP, population,
and life expectancy in order to support indicator-level forecasting and model comparison.
Three main GDP models were implemented using an interpretable econometric framework
in statsmodels. Model 1 uses population and life expectancy as baseline predictors.
Model 2 extends the baseline by adding inflation, unemployment, and internet usage. Model
3 further incorporates global event dummies, region effects, and a time trend for
future-oriented forecasting. For the supporting time-series stage, rolling 10-year backtesting
was used to compare alternative models. The best-performing models were Naive for GDP,
Naive for life expectancy, and LogHolt for population. For the main GDP models, in-sample
fit improved progressively from Model 1 (R² = 0.6950) to Model 2 (R² = 0.7484) and
Model 3 (R² = 0.7983). A shared train-test comparison also showed that Model 3 was the
strongest overall specification on the held-out period.
The analytical outputs were integrated into a Streamlit dashboard for multi-indicator
exploration, model comparison, rolling backtest inspection, and future forecast visualisation.
Overall, the project demonstrates how interpretable data science methods can be used to
combine exploratory analysis, time-series forecasting, panel-style regression, and interactive
reporting in a coherent GDP-focused decision-support workflow.
Keywords: GDP per capita, forecasting, time series, panel data, regression, ARIMA, Holt,
AutoReg, Streamlit, World Bank.
Chapter 1. Introduction
1.1 Background
Economic performance differs widely across countries and regions due to variation in
demographic structure, labour productivity, health outcomes, macroeconomic stability,
technological development, and institutional conditions. GDP per capita is one of the most
widely used indicators in economic analysis because it provides a concise measure of output
relative to population size. In contrast to total GDP, GDP per capita is more suitable for
analysing living standards, productivity, and long-term development outcomes because it
reflects average economic output per person rather than aggregate national scale.
Nevertheless, GDP per capita does not change independently of other socio-economic
conditions. Population affects labour supply, market size, and dependency structure. Life
expectancy reflects broader human capital and health conditions. Inflation and
unemployment capture macroeconomic instability and labour market weakness. Internet
usage acts as a proxy for digital access and the broader process of economic
modernisation. In addition, major global shocks such as financial crises, pandemic
disruption, and energy shocks can alter national economic trajectories in ways that are not
captured by smooth trend variables alone.
From a data science perspective, GDP forecasting is a strong final-year project topic
because it combines multiple technical stages: data collection, cleaning, feature engineering,
exploratory analysis, forecasting, statistical modelling, evaluation, and deployment. It also
enables a useful balance between interpretability and predictive power, which is especially
important in an academic setting where methods must be both explainable and defensible.
1.2 Problem Statement
Although GDP forecasting has been widely studied, many simplified academic
implementations either focus only on one variable as a univariate time series or apply
black-box machine learning methods without sufficient interpretability. For a final-year
project, this creates a methodological challenge. The project must be sufficiently technical to
demonstrate analytical ability, yet sufficiently transparent to support academic explanation,
evaluation, and defence.
More specifically, this study addresses three related problems. First, it seeks to understand
how GDP per capita evolves across countries and regions over time. Second, it investigates
whether population, life expectancy, inflation, unemployment, internet usage, and global
event structure improve the prediction of next-year GDP per capita. Third, it explores how
analytical outputs can be translated into a dashboard that supports interpretation and
demonstration.
A further challenge lies in distinguishing clearly between two modelling tasks. The first is
indicator-level forecasting, where GDP, population, and life expectancy are each treated as
separate time series. The second is GDP prediction modelling, where several demographic
and macroeconomic indicators are used together to estimate future GDP per capita. This
distinction is essential for methodological clarity.
1.3 Aim
The aim of this project is to analyse global GDP per capita trends, investigate the
relationship between GDP per capita and selected demographic and macroeconomic
indicators, and develop interpretable forecasting models that predict future GDP per capita
using open international data.
1.4 Objectives
The objectives of the project are as follows:
1. To collect and prepare open country-level datasets for GDP per capita, population,
life expectancy, inflation, unemployment, internet usage, and country metadata.
2. To conduct exploratory data analysis on GDP per capita and related indicators across
countries, regions, and years.
3. To engineer a country-year panel dataset with additional global event dummy
variables.
4. To build and compare separate time-series models for GDP, population, and life
expectancy using rolling 10-year backtesting.
5. To construct three interpretable GDP models of increasing complexity, from a simple
baseline to a fuller macroeconomic and structural specification.
6. To evaluate model behaviour using standard metrics such as MAE, RMSE, MAPE,
and R²
.
7. To present the analytical results through an interactive Streamlit dashboard.
1.5 Research Questions
This report is organised around the following research questions:
1. What long-term patterns are visible in GDP per capita, population, and life

2. Which time-series models perform best for GDP, population, and life expectancy

3. How much explanatory and predictive improvement is obtained when moving from a

1.6 Scope of the Study
This project focuses on annual country-level data derived mainly from World Bank-style
open datasets. The study is limited to GDP per capita as the primary economic target,
interpretable modelling techniques implemented in Python, and dashboard deployment
through Streamlit. The work does not attempt to make strict causal claims. Instead, it
focuses on association, modelling usefulness, and forecasting-oriented interpretation.
Although more advanced machine learning techniques such as Random Forest, XGBoost, or
LSTM could be explored, the current project prioritises transparency and academic
explainability over black-box predictive complexity.

Chapter 2. Literature Review
2.1 Introduction to the Literature Review
Forecasting GDP per capita sits at the intersection of economic growth theory,
macroeconomic modelling, time-series forecasting, and applied data science. A strong
literature review for this project must therefore go beyond a narrow discussion of algorithms
and instead position the study within broader theoretical and empirical debates. In particular,
the report needs to explain why GDP per capita is an appropriate target, why the selected
indicators are relevant, why interpretable models are valuable, and why structural shocks
should be represented explicitly in the modelling design.
This chapter reviews the literature in five main layers. First, it outlines the economic theory
behind growth, productivity, and per-capita income. Second, it reviews the literature linking
demographic and macroeconomic variables to development outcomes. Third, it discusses
forecasting methods relevant to annual macroeconomic data. Fourth, it evaluates the role of
structural breaks and global shocks in growth modelling. Finally, it explains the
methodological gap that the current project addresses.
2.2 Economic Growth Theory and GDP per Capita
GDP per capita is widely used in economics because it captures average output per person
and therefore functions as a practical indicator of productive capacity and living standards. In
growth theory, long-run income differences are commonly linked to capital accumulation,
labour force dynamics, productivity, technological change, and human capital formation.
Solow’s (1956) neoclassical growth model established a foundational framework in which
output depends on capital, labour, and exogenous technological progress. Although the
Solow model does not directly use variables such as life expectancy or internet usage, it
provides the underlying logic for why long-run per-capita output reflects deeper structural
conditions.
Later work extended this framework by incorporating human capital more explicitly. Mankiw,
Romer and Weil (1992) argued that cross-country growth patterns are better explained when
human capital is added to the traditional Solow framework. This extension is directly relevant
to the current project because life expectancy can be treated as a broad proxy for human
development and labour quality, while internet usage can be interpreted as a proxy for
technological access and modernisation.
Endogenous growth theory also strengthened the case for including broader socio-economic
variables in growth models. Romer (1990) emphasised the role of knowledge, innovation,
and technological change, suggesting that productivity differences across countries cannot
always be explained by factor accumulation alone. In practical forecasting terms, this
supports the inclusion of variables that reflect broader development capacity rather than
relying only on scale variables such as population.
2.3 Population, Labour, and Per-Capita Output
Population has a complex relationship with economic performance. On the one hand, a
larger population can expand labour supply, domestic markets, and aggregate production.
On the other hand, GDP per capita is not determined by total population alone. A country
may have a very large population but still have low output per person if productivity,
infrastructure, institutions, or human capital are weak.
This distinction is important because it explains why the current project models GDP per
capita rather than total GDP. In aggregate growth analysis, population is often positively
linked to total output, but in per-capita modelling the relationship may weaken or even
change sign after controlling for other factors. This is consistent with the economic logic that
scale alone does not guarantee high average income. In cross-country work, population is
therefore better interpreted as a structural context variable than as a simple direct driver of
per-capita prosperity.
The decision to use log_population_total in the current project is also supported by
standard econometric practice. Log transformations reduce extreme scale differences and
make the relationship more stable across small and large economies.
2.4 Human Capital, Health, and Life Expectancy
Human capital has long been recognised as a key determinant of economic development.
Schultz (1961) and Becker (1964) argued that investments in human capabilities, including
education and health, improve productivity and long-run economic performance. Although
these classic studies focused more broadly on human capital, they provide a strong
conceptual basis for including life expectancy in a GDP model.
Life expectancy is not a direct measure of skill, but it is a meaningful development indicator
because it reflects public health, institutional quality, nutrition, healthcare access, and
long-term welfare conditions. Bloom, Canning and Sevilla (2004) showed that health
improvements can contribute positively to economic growth by improving labour productivity,
reducing mortality-related losses, and supporting stronger human capital accumulation. In
applied country-level modelling, life expectancy therefore serves as a practical proxy for
broader development capacity.
For the present study, the inclusion of life expectancy is especially useful because it links
demographic analysis with economic forecasting. Unlike some purely economic variables
that are highly volatile, life expectancy tends to evolve gradually, which makes it both
theoretically meaningful and empirically stable.
2.5 Inflation, Unemployment, and Macroeconomic
Stability
Macroeconomic stability is a central theme in growth and development research. Fischer
(1993) argued that inflation, budget imbalance, and external instability are associated with
weaker growth performance. Although inflation is not always uniformly harmful in every
short-run context, persistent inflation often signals macroeconomic imbalance, reduced
confidence, and weaker real purchasing power. As a result, inflation is a reasonable
explanatory variable in a GDP forecasting model.
Unemployment is also relevant, though its interpretation is more complex. Okun (1962)
highlighted the relationship between output and unemployment, suggesting that labour
underutilisation tends to accompany weaker economic performance. However, in
cross-country pooled models, unemployment can behave differently from a simple short-run
macroeconomic theory because it is influenced by labour market institutions, income level,
measurement quality, and structural transformation. This is why unemployment may produce
counterintuitive coefficients in some multivariate settings. The variable remains important,
but it must be interpreted cautiously and not causally.
Taken together, inflation and unemployment provide a macroeconomic stability lens that
complements demographic and development indicators. Their inclusion in the current project
helps move the model from a purely demographic baseline toward a more realistic
macroeconomic specification.
2.6 Digital Access and Economic Development
Digital access has become increasingly important in development analysis because
information and communication technologies affect productivity, connectivity, knowledge
diffusion, and market participation. The World Bank (2016), in the World Development
Report: Digital Dividends, argued that digital technologies can enhance economic inclusion
and efficiency, although their benefits depend on complementary institutions and skills.
Similarly, research on broadband and digital infrastructure has shown that digital expansion
may contribute positively to productivity and growth, especially in more connected
economies.
In the present project, internet_users_pct_clean is used as a practical proxy for digital
access and technological diffusion. It is not a complete measure of digital capability, but it
captures an important dimension of structural modernisation that is often missing from
traditional GDP models. Its inclusion is especially valuable because it adds a
development-oriented variable that bridges economics and technology.
2.7 Time-Series Forecasting for Annual Macro Data
Time-series modelling remains central to economic forecasting because many
macroeconomic indicators exhibit persistence, gradual change, and structural shocks over
time. Hamilton (1994) provided one of the foundational treatments of time-series analysis in
economics, while Hyndman and Athanasopoulos (2021) offered a modern applied
framework covering Naive forecasting, autoregressive models, ARIMA, exponential
smoothing, and evaluation methods.
In annual cross-country macroeconomic data, simple models often remain surprisingly
competitive. A Naive model may perform strongly when year-to-year changes are small
relative to the overall level of the indicator. Autoregressive models are useful when
short-term memory is informative, while ARIMA can capture differencing and stochastic
structure in more dynamic series. Exponential smoothing models, such as Holt’s method, are
particularly effective when the series follows a smooth trend.
The current project adopts this classical time-series logic for GDP, population, and life
expectancy separately. This is methodologically appropriate because the three indicators do
not behave in the same way. The population tends to move smoothly over time and is well
suited to log-trend smoothing. Life expectancy often follows a gradual development path.
GDP per capita can be more volatile, but still exhibits persistence strong enough for simple
baselines to perform well in one-step-ahead forecasting.
2.8 Panel-Style Regression and Interpretable GDP
Prediction
Regression-based models remain widely used in applied econometrics because they
combine prediction with interpretability. In the context of GDP-related modelling, regression
enables researchers to explain how multiple indicators are associated with future economic
outcomes while still producing usable forecasts. Compared with purely black-box methods, a
regression framework makes it easier to justify variable choice, interpret coefficients, and
discuss limitations.
The current project uses next-year log GDP per capita as the target variable. This modelling
choice is justified for three reasons. First, predicting GDP_(t+1) is more aligned with
forecasting logic than predicting same-year GDP from same-year inputs. Second, the log
transformation reduces the influence of extreme values and compresses the distribution
across rich and poor economies. Third, the transformed target supports more stable
coefficient estimation across a highly heterogeneous international panel.
This progression from a simple baseline model to richer multivariate specifications is
consistent with good modelling practice in applied research. Rather than beginning with the
most complex specification, the report uses three models to show how explanatory power
changes as additional variables and structural features are introduced.
2.9 Structural Breaks, Crises, and Global Event
Dummies
One of the major limitations of smooth forecasting models is that they often struggle when
historical data contain major shocks or regime changes. Financial crises, pandemics, wars,
and energy shocks can create abrupt deviations from ordinary economic patterns. In such
settings, structural break awareness becomes essential.
The literature on forecasting under structural change suggests that model performance may
deteriorate if crisis periods are treated as ordinary observations. In practical applied work,
one common response is to include dummy variables for historically exceptional periods.
While event dummies do not fully solve the problem of causal identification, they offer a
transparent way to represent major disturbances in a forecasting-oriented regression.
This logic strongly supports the inclusion of global event dummies in the present project. The
Asian Financial Crisis, Global Financial Crisis, COVID shock, COVID rebound, and
Ukraine-energy shock period were all large macroeconomic disturbances with international
spillover effects. Representing them explicitly allows the model to distinguish ordinary
development patterns from globally disruptive episodes.
2.10 Interpretable Models Versus Black-Box Prediction
Recent forecasting studies increasingly apply machine learning methods such as Random
Forest, XGBoost, support vector regression, and deep learning. These approaches can
model non-linear relationships and interactions more flexibly than classical econometric
models. However, their advantages come with trade-offs. In academic work, especially at
undergraduate final-project level, highly complex models can reduce interpretability,
complicate justification, and make results more difficult to defend.
For the current project, interpretability is not a weakness but a design principle. The project
is intended not only to generate predictions, but also to explain why the predictions behave
as they do. This makes classical time-series models and interpretable regression
frameworks especially appropriate. They allow the study to evaluate model quality while
preserving clear connections between theory, variables, and results.

2.12 Chapter Summary
The literature shows that GDP per capita is shaped by multiple structural and

Chapter 3. Methodology
3.1 Research Design
This study follows an applied quantitative design. It combines exploratory data analysis,
time-series forecasting, regression-based GDP modelling, and interactive dashboard
deployment. The workflow was intentionally organised in stages so that each part supports
the next: data collection, cleaning, panel construction, exploratory analysis, separate
time-series modelling, multivariate GDP modelling, and final deployment.
This design supports reproducibility because each stage produces intermediate outputs that
are reused later. It also supports academic clarity because the report can distinguish
between descriptive analysis, indicator-level forecasting, and GDP prediction modelling.
3.2 Data Sources
The project uses annual country-level datasets for GDP per capita, population, life
expectancy, inflation, unemployment, individuals using the Internet, and World Bank country
metadata. These datasets were selected because they provide broad cross-country
coverage and allow GDP per capita to be analysed together with demographic,
macroeconomic, and technological indicators.
3.3 Data Preparation and Panel Construction
The raw datasets were originally stored in wide format and then transformed into long
country-year format. They were merged by country_code and year, after which World
Bank regional metadata were attached. Additional engineered variables were then created,
including log transformations and next-year GDP targets.
The final extended panel contains 11,373 rows, 25 columns, 214 countries, 7 World Bank
regions, and the year range 1960 to 2023. However, the sample used for the richer GDP
models is smaller because some macroeconomic variables are incomplete. After listwise
deletion for the full set of required predictors, the main GDP modelling subset contains 4,722
observations over the period 1991 to 2022.

5.3 GDP Time-Series Results
For GDP per capita, the Naive model outperformed ARIMA and AutoReg in the rolling
10-year evaluation. This suggests that annual GDP per capita is highly persistent and that
the previous year often provides a strong one-step-ahead benchmark. In this setting, more
complex models did not consistently improve accuracy across countries.
This finding is not unusual in macroeconomic forecasting. When data are annual and
country-specific histories are not extremely long, simple persistence models can remain
highly competitive. Therefore, the strong performance of the Naive model should be
interpreted as an empirical result rather than as evidence of methodological weakness.
Figure 5.1. Best-model GDP forecast versus actual values for Monaco under rolling
10-year backtesting.
Figure 5. shows the country-level behaviour of the selected GDP time-series model against
the actual GDP per capita series. The forecasted series broadly follows the long-run upward
movement of GDP per capita, indicating that the selected model captures the general level
and direction of economic change reasonably well. However, the visual gap between the
predicted and actual series is still larger than in the life expectancy and population cases,
especially around sharper movements and local turning points.
This pattern is consistent with the earlier evaluation results showing that GDP is the most
volatile of the three core series. Even when the Naive model performs best overall, it tends
to lag sudden increases or decreases because it projects the next year largely from the most
recent observed value. Therefore, the figure confirms an important methodological point of
the project: GDP is forecastable to a useful degree, but its dynamics remain more sensitive
to shocks and short-run fluctuations than the demographic indicators.
Figure 5.1. GDP time-series model comparison under rolling backtesting.
5.4 Life Expectancy Time-Series Results
Life expectancy also favoured the Naive model, with Holt performing slightly worse and
AutoReg performing much more poorly. This is consistent with the smooth and gradual
nature of life expectancy change. In many countries, life expectancy evolves through slow
structural improvement rather than abrupt annual fluctuation. As a result, the most recent
observed value is often a strong predictor of the next year.
Although Holt is still a reasonable choice for smooth extrapolation, the backtest results show
that Naive provides the most reliable one-step-ahead performance in the current data.
Figure 5.. Best-model life expectancy forecast versus actual values for Andorra under
rolling 10-year backtesting.
Figure 5. demonstrates that the selected life expectancy model tracks the observed series
very closely across most of the historical period. The predicted line almost overlaps with the
actual line, which visually confirms the strong backtesting performance reported earlier.
Deviations are generally small and become more noticeable only around abrupt
disturbances, particularly during the pandemic-related disruption period.
This result is methodologically intuitive. Life expectancy usually evolves gradually through
long-run structural improvement rather than through sharp year-to-year volatility. As a result,
a simple model such as Naive can perform very well in one-step-ahead forecasting because
the most recent observation already contains substantial predictive information about the
next year. The figure therefore supports the conclusion that life expectancy is the smoothest
and most stable of the three main time-series considered in the project.
5.5 Population Time-Series Results
Population forecasting produced a different pattern. The best-performing model was
LogHolt, with exceptionally strong results across MAE, RMSE, MAPE, and R²
. This is
methodologically intuitive because population usually changes through gradual
compounding growth rather than short-run volatility. A log-scale smoothing model is
therefore well suited to this type of demographic series.
The much weaker performance of AutoReg further suggests that short-lag autoregressive
structure is less useful than long-run smoothed trend for annual population data.
Figure 5.6. Best-model population forecast versus actual values for China under
rolling 10-year backtesting.
Figure 5.6 shows an extremely close alignment between the predicted and actual population
series, with the two lines remaining nearly indistinguishable across most years. This
provides strong visual support for the excellent quantitative performance of the LogHolt
model reported in the model comparison section. The selected model captures both the
long-run upward movement and the gradual flattening of the series very effectively.
The result is also theoretically consistent with the nature of annual population data.
Population tends to change through slow compounding growth rather than abrupt short-run
variation, making it well suited to a smoothed log-scale trend model. Compared with GDP,
the population series is much more predictable; compared with life expectancy, it combines
smoothness with large-scale cumulative growth. The figure therefore reinforces the
conclusion that LogHolt is highly appropriate for population forecasting in this project.
5.6 Role of the Time-Series Stage in the Full Workflow
The separate indicator models are not isolated sub-tasks. They serve a direct purpose in the
full GDP forecasting workflow because they can be used to generate future values of
population, life expectancy, inflation, unemployment, and internet usage before those values
are passed into the GDP models. In other words, the time-series stage supports the
construction of future feature tables, which are then used to estimate GDP per capita for a
user-selected future target year.
5.7 Chapter Summary
The time-series results show that indicator behaviour differs substantially across datasets.
GDP and life expectancy are well represented by simple persistence, while population is
better represented by smoothed log-trend growth. These findings support the project’s
decision to model the indicators separately before combining them in the main GDP
forecasting framework.

6.3 Interpretation of Key Coefficients
Several patterns in the coefficient tables are analytically meaningful.
First, life_expectancy_years remains positive and highly significant across the main
models. This supports the broader literature linking health and human development to
stronger economic performance. In this context, life expectancy appears to be a robust proxy
for underlying development capacity.
Second, log_population_total is negative in the GDP per capita models. This should
not be misread as a claim that population harms the economy. Because the target is GDP
per capita rather than total GDP, the negative sign simply indicates that larger population
size alone does not guarantee higher output per person once other variables are controlled
for.
Third, internet_users_pct_clean is positive and strongly significant in Models 2 and 3.
This is consistent with the descriptive evidence that digital access is associated with more
advanced development conditions and, potentially, stronger productivity.
Fourth, inflation_pct_clean is negative in the richer models, which aligns with the
argument that macroeconomic instability tends to be associated with weaker per-capita
output performance.
Finally, unemployment_pct_clean is positive in the fitted models, which appears
counterintuitive. This is one of the clearest reminders that the model should not be
interpreted causally. In a pooled international panel, sign reversals may arise due to
multicollinearity, omitted institutional differences, labour market structure, or cross-country
composition effects.
6.4 Region Effects and Structural Heterogeneity
The inclusion of region effects in Model 3 is justified both descriptively and statistically. The
exploratory analysis showed substantial regional differences in GDP per capita and life
expectancy, and the fuller model confirms that these structural differences remain relevant
even after controlling for the included predictors.
This is important because a purely pooled model without region structure risks blending
together development patterns that differ sharply across high-income, middle-income, and
low-income regions. By including C(wb_region), Model 3 acknowledges that countries do
not operate within a uniform economic environment.
6.5 Interpretation of Event Dummies
The event terms must be discussed carefully. In the full-sample analysis, event dummies
improve explanatory fit because they help capture historically unusual periods. However, in
the strict train-test evaluation, some event variables such as the COVID shock and the
Ukraine-energy shock lie outside the training window. This means they cannot be learned in
the usual way from pre-2018 training data.
This creates an important academic distinction. In full-sample modelling, event dummies
function as explanatory historical controls. In genuine future forecasting, however, the effect
of future shocks cannot be estimated directly unless similar events exist in the training data.
In practice, such variables must be treated as scenario assumptions rather than ordinary
predictors. This is an important strength of the report because it demonstrates
methodological awareness rather than overclaiming forecasting ability.
6.6 Regional Test Behaviour
The shared test evaluation also shows that performance varies by region. Model 3
performed relatively well in South Asia, East Asia and Pacific, and the Middle East, North
Africa, Afghanistan and Pakistan group. However, performance remained weaker in North
America and parts of Sub-Saharan Africa, where either the sample is small or the regional
structure is highly heterogeneous. This indicates that even the strongest pooled model
cannot perfectly represent every regional pattern.
6.7 Overall Interpretation of the Main Models
Taken together, the evidence suggests a clear hierarchy. Model 1 is useful as a simple,
interpretable baseline. Model 2 strengthens predictive performance by adding
macroeconomic and digital indicators. Model 3 is the strongest overall specification because
it combines demographic, macroeconomic, technological, regional, and event-based
structure within one forecasting-oriented framework.
6.8 Chapter Summary
The main-model results show that broader macroeconomic and structural information
improves both explanatory fit and out-of-sample predictive quality. Model 3 is the best overall
candidate for the final report and demo, while Models 1 and 2 remain important for
comparative justification and progressive model development.

Chapter 8. Critical Evaluation,
Limitations, and Future Work
8.1 Strengths of the Project
The project has several strengths. It integrates data engineering, exploratory analysis,
time-series forecasting, regression modelling, and dashboard deployment within one
coherent workflow. It distinguishes clearly between indicator-level forecasting and
multivariate GDP prediction. It prioritises interpretability, which is valuable in an academic
setting. It also incorporates global event dummies and region effects, which give the model
more economic realism than a purely mechanical specification.
8.2 Limitations
Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged.
First, missingness in unemployment and internet usage substantially reduces the usable
sample for the richer models. Second, the GDP models are pooled OLS specifications rather
than full country fixed-effects or dynamic panel models. Third, the event dummies are
manually engineered from historically known periods and therefore depend partly on
researcher judgement. Fourth, MAPE is unstable when actual GDP per capita values are
very small. Fifth, future shocks cannot always be learned directly from a pre-shock training
sample and must therefore be treated as scenario assumptions.
These limitations do not invalidate the findings, but they define the scope of what the study
can claim.
8.3 Why Black-Box Machine Learning Was Not the Core
Choice
Methods such as Random Forest, XGBoost, Prophet, or LSTM could have been included as
advanced benchmarks. However, the current project intentionally prioritises models that are
easier to explain, justify, and defend. For a final-year academic project, interpretability is
especially important because the report must not only present results, but also explain how
and why those results emerge.
8.4 Future Improvements
Several future extensions could strengthen the work further:

8.5 Critical Reflection
A key lesson of the project is that forecasting quality depends not only on model complexity,
but also on data quality, variable definition, evaluation design, and the distinction between
historical fit and genuine future uncertainty. In this sense, the project is academically
valuable because it demonstrates methodological awareness rather than merely numerical
optimisation.
Chapter 9. Conclusion
This project developed an interpretable GDP per capita forecasting workflow using
demographic, macroeconomic, technological, and event-based information from open
international datasets. It combined three major strands of work: exploratory analysis of
country-year indicators, separate rolling time-series modelling for GDP, population, and life
expectancy, and progressive GDP regression models ranging from a simple baseline to a
fuller forecasting-oriented specification.
The results show that separate indicator forecasting is both feasible and informative. Under
a rolling 10-year backtest design, the best models were Naive for GDP, Naive for life
expectancy, and LogHolt for population. For the main GDP models, both in-sample and
shared test evidence show that Model 3 is the strongest overall specification. It delivers the
best balance of demographic, macroeconomic, regional, and event-aware information while
remaining interpretable.
At the same time, the report also makes clear that forecasting in this setting has limitations.
Missing data reduce the usable sample for richer models, pooled international structure
complicates coefficient interpretation, and major future shocks cannot always be inferred
from historical patterns alone. These limitations are not weaknesses to hide; rather, they are
part of a rigorous academic interpretation of the modelling process.
Overall, the project meets its aim of building a transparent, academically defensible, and
practically demonstrable GDP forecasting system. It also provides a strong foundation for
future development through richer econometric methods, scenario analysis, uncertainty
estimation, and machine learning benchmarking.
